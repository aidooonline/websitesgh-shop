# The anti-leak playbook: making every cedi land on a page that converts

## The lesson from the Imani rentals leak

A search campaign bled money because keywords pulled traffic the landing page
could not satisfy: someone searched a rental term, hit a sales page, bounced.
The click was paid for, nothing gained. The same thing kills appliance
campaigns when you bid on bare head terms.

## The one rule: keyword-to-page match

A keyword earns a place in the account ONLY if a person typing it would be
happy landing on your exact page. Three tests, all must pass:

1. INTENT: are they buying, or researching, fixing, comparing, renting? Keep
   only buyers. This is why every keyword we kept contains a buy verb (buy,
   for sale, where to buy, price) or a commercial modifier (price in ghana,
   accra). No bare "blender", "microwave", "iron" - those pull everyone.
2. PRODUCT: do you actually stock the exact thing? If the query names a brand
   or spec you do not carry, the click is wasted. Add such brands as negatives
   the moment they show in the search terms report.
3. GEO/SITUATION: does the page serve their case, Ghana, delivery, price shown?
   Our pages do; keywords carry ghana/accra so the match is explicit.

Result of applying the rule: the list went from 621 keywords to 91. Fewer
keywords, dramatically higher qualified-click rate. This is correct. Volume is
the enemy of a lean launch budget.

## Match type discipline (why we do NOT use broad)

- EXACT ([keyword]): only the near-exact query triggers it. Highest intent,
  lowest waste. Used on the strongest buy terms.
- PHRASE ("keyword"): the query must contain your phrase in order. Good for
  the price/ghana variants. Some flexibility, still guarded.
- BROAD: NONE at launch. Broad match hands Google the right to match loosely,
  which without a strong conversion signal is exactly how the rentals leak
  happens. We add broad ONLY later, for one proven ad group, watched daily,
  once Smart Bidding has real sale data to steer it.

## The 107 negative keywords are half the defense

Loaded day one. They pre-block the non-buyers before they ever cost you:
free/used/tokunbo, repair/service, how-to/review/specs researchers,
wholesale/importer/job seekers, competitor and marketplace names (jumia, jiji,
melcom), rentals and wrong-geography terms. Full list in
negative-keywords.csv.

## The weekly ritual that keeps it clean (this is where the 80% is won)

Keywords are a starting guess. The SEARCH TERMS REPORT is the truth. Every
week, without fail:

1. Google Ads > the campaign > Insights > Search terms.
2. Read every actual query that triggered your ads.
3. For each query that is NOT a real buyer for a product you stock:
   add it as a negative keyword immediately.
4. For each query that IS a great buyer and converted: add it as an exact
   keyword in its ad group, so you bid on it precisely next time.
5. Check cost-per-conversion per ad group. Any group above your profit per
   order (~GHS 105) for two weeks: pause it, move budget to winners.

Do this for 4 weeks and the account self-cleans toward 80%+ qualified traffic.
The first two weeks WILL show junk queries; that is normal, and the ritual is
how you starve them. Nobody launches at 80%; you prune to 80%.

## Landing page must match the ad (the other half of the leak)

A qualified click still wastes money if the page does not deliver. Our pages
already do this: the product page opens with the price and pay-on-delivery,
the two order buttons, the honest spec. The category page shows the range with
WhatsApp on every card. Keep it that way: the ad promises a price and delivery,
the page must show a price and delivery ABOVE the fold. If you ever change a
landing page, re-check that the ad's promise is still the first thing seen.

## Quality Score: the compounding reward

When keyword, ad copy and landing page all match tightly (which is why the ad
groups are single-theme), Google raises your Quality Score, and a high Quality
Score can cut your real CPC by up to half. So this discipline does not just
raise conversion, it lowers the price of every click. It compounds.
